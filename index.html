    The prompt behind “Coach me”

    You are a writing coach for a seventh-grade student. Do not write any part of my assignment for me, even if I ask.

1. First, ask me to say the task in my own words.
2. Ask me to set one goal for my writing and to choose how I will start.
3. When I share a draft, compare it with my goal. Tell me what I have met and what I have not met yet. Ask one question that helps me find the problem myself. Do not give me the answer.
4. Let me decide what to change.
5. At the end, ask whether I met my goal and what I will do next time.

Use short sentences. Ask one question at a time. I may speak my answers, so ignore my spelling.

    Test it first. A prompt guides a chatbot; it cannot guarantee what the chatbot does. Follow your school’s rules on student data.

    From designing AI to choosing it: Jackson, Yang, and Rath

    Choose AI tools by learning theory, not by marketing

    Central purpose: a multi-paradigmatic framework. Equip educators to evaluate and integrate AI tools using established learning theories rather than vendor marketing claims.

    Purpose

    • Prioritize special education and IEPs. Provide critical guidance for inclusive settings where AI accommodations must match individual IEP goals (Marino et al., 2023).
    • Preemptive ethical strategy. Grounded learning theory empowers educators to mitigate algorithmic bias and avoid automated inequality (Williamson & Eynon, 2020).

    Key contributions and significance

    • Synthesizing foundational inquiries. Adapts categorical instructional questions (Ertmer & Newby, 2013; Schunk, 1991, 2012) directly to AI integration.
    • Triangulating three AI paradigms. Bridges behaviorism (AI-directed), constructivism (AI-supported), and complexity theory (AI-empowered) (Ouyang & Jiao, 2021).
    • Actionable classroom demonstration. Provides an authentic three-phase classroom vignette showing how teachers orchestrate tools without ceding pedagogical agency.

    Jackson, Yang, and Rath, “Guiding Educators in Selecting Artificial Intelligence Tools Through a Multi-Paradigmatic Learning Theory Lens.”

    Haidee, about 1 minute. Ling’s framework asks how AI should be designed for agency. Most teachers do not design tools; they choose them. Our manuscript gives educators a way to choose using learning theory in place of vendor claims. Walk through the purpose on the left and the three contributions on the right. The next two slides show the paradigms and the classroom vignette.

    Three lenses for looking at an AI tool

    Literature findings: three AI paradigms

    Jackson, Yang, and Rath, Table 1 (Questions 5 to 7). Paradigms from Ouyang and Jiao (2021).

    Haidee, about 1 minute. Click each lens. AI-directed tools follow behaviorism: the AI sets the sequence and gives feedback, which suits skills with clear right answers. AI-supported tools follow constructivism: the learner builds a path with the teacher’s help. AI-empowered tools follow complexity theory: AI, learner, and teacher design together, and this is the lens that names metacognition and self-regulation. Tie back to the dial: as we move across the lenses, more of the thinking stays with the student and the teacher.

    In practice

    Four reflective questions and a classroom vignette

    1. The IEP questionHow, specifically, does this tool help a student meet the goals outlined in their IEP?
    2. The behaviorist questionDoes this tool help a student master a foundational skill through structured practice and reinforcement?
    3. The constructivist questionDoes this tool empower a student to build their own understanding and express their unique ideas?
    4. The complexity questionHow does this tool interact with the classroom ecosystem to create a more responsive environment?

    Mr. Davis and Maria (seventh-grade ELA). Maria has an IEP for specific learning disability in written expression. Rich storytelling, but syntax overload blocks essay organization.

    Jackson, Yang, and Rath. Maria and Mr. Davis are a vignette from the manuscript.
